tldr:
  - Designed a walk-in hiring poster that had to carry eleven mandatory facts and still scan on a phone.
  - Explored 12 directions over 3 rounds, then refined the winning bento grid over 6 versions.
  - Built one portrait master and adapted it to landscape and square without a redesign.
  - Audited a late content change against UX laws and lifted the layout from 6/10 to 9/10.
  - The marketer ran the designs as Meta and LinkedIn ads, and around 200 to 300 people walked in over three days.
context:
  problem: "Colaberry needed to hire Python backend developers quickly: 4+ years of experience, immediate joiners, a three-day walk-in at T-Hub, Hyderabad, with the job based at the ECIL office. The poster had to carry role, experience, skills, dates, time window, venue, work location, employment mode, what to bring, a registration QR and contact numbers. Every one of those had to be found by someone glancing at a phone, and it had to print at the venue too."
  business: "The hiring team ran the drive, and I design across Colaberry's marketing and business teams. The poster was built on the Colaberry Business design system: teal for trust and structure, cyan for energy and calls to action, plain copy and no emoji."
  constraints:
    - Eleven mandatory facts, none of them optional.
    - Work on screen (LinkedIn, WhatsApp, ads) and in print at the venue.
    - "Colaberry Business system: teal primary, cyan accent, one icon set, no emoji."
    - Body text at 4.5:1 contrast or better on every surface, including the dark variant and the teal cards.
    - The venue (T-Hub) and the work location (ECIL) were different places, and both had to be clear.
research:
  summary: The hard problem was density, not style. The layout that gave every fact its own place was the one that survived every format change.
  insights:
    - Dates-as-hero layouts looked strong but left the other facts competing for one block of text.
    - The bento grid was the only direction where all eleven facts had a home.
    - Full-bleed band layouts read well at poster size but forced top-to-bottom reading, which hurt the quick scan on a phone.
    - A late addition, "4+ years experience", broke the grouping when it was dropped in as an extra pill.
process:
  summary: Diverge three times, converge once, then audit.
  steps:
    - title: Five directions, one question
      body: 'Round 1 answered "what is the hero?" five ways: dates in light and dark, an event-pass ticket, a bento grid, and a strict variant with cyan only on the call to action. The bento grid won, then went through three refinements that added light and dark variants and an icon set.'
    - title: Run a challenger
      body: Round 2 tested two full-bleed band layouts, in case the grid was winning on familiarity. They confirmed the grid instead of replacing it.
    - title: Remix a reference
      body: Round 3 rebuilt a supplied reference poster in Colaberry's system twice. Its split hero over the Round 1 bento grid became the final structure.
    - title: Build once, adapt to every format
      body: I built the portrait master as HTML in three zones (hero, bento, footer), then adapted it to landscape and square ads. Every format was exported at 2x, 2160 by 2700 for portrait.
    - title: Audit the late change
      body: A late request added "4+ years experience" to the hero. I scored it against UX laws at 6/10, regrouped the pills, moved work location into the mode card and replaced a repeated footer line, reaching 9/10.
decisions:
  - decision: Chunk the facts into a bento grid instead of making the dates the hero.
    tradeoff: A less dramatic first impression than a giant date wall, in exchange for every fact being findable at a glance.
  - decision: Run a challenger round after a favourite had already emerged.
    tradeoff: One extra round of work. It removed the doubt that the grid was winning only because it was familiar.
  - decision: Move the work location out of the hero and into the mode card, so the hero holds three chunks.
    tradeoff: The difference between the T-Hub venue and the ECIL work location still takes a second read.
  - decision: "Keep the accent scarce: one cyan element per zone."
    tradeoff: Less overall energy on the page, but the primary role and the call to action are the first things the eye finds.
outcomes:
  summary: The marketer ran these designs as Meta and LinkedIn ads, and around 200 to 300 people walked in over the three days. Turnout also depended on ad spend, targeting and the roles on offer, so this is the campaign result, not the design alone. Hires from the drive are not counted here yet.

quote: Give every fact a home first. Style is easier once nothing is fighting for space.
reflection:
  summary: A dense event poster is an information design problem. A grid that gives every fact its own region survives every format and every late change.
  learnings:
    - Chunk dense facts into regions before styling them.
    - Run a challenger direction even after a favourite emerges.
    - Audit late additions. They are the changes most likely to break the grouping.
    - Keep the accent scarce, so the call to action stays findable.
    - Next time I would settle the venue and work location wording with the hiring team at the brief, so it does not need a second read.
